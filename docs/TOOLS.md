# Tools

The small jobs that sit around the calculator. Two rules run through all of
them: the answer shows its working, and nothing is invented.

## Colour schemes

A colour wheel will happily hand back `#7B4FA3`. No manufacturer sells
`#7B4FA3`. So the wheel is used only to decide *where* a companion shade
belongs, and what comes back is the nearest real shade from a manufacturer's
own card — brand, product, shade name, code — together with how far it actually
falls from that ideal. The ideal is shown beside it as a small square, so the
gap is visible rather than hidden. A scheme that looks perfect on screen and
cannot be bought is worse than no scheme at all.

The distances are CIEDE2000 in CIELAB, the standard way of asking "would a
person call these two the same colour". Under 2 is a match; under about 12 two
shades read as one in stitches, which is fine for a subtle stripe and no good
for a face; the contrast ratio alongside says whether a muzzle will show up
against a head across a room.

The schemes are the usual ones — complementary, analogous, triadic, split,
square, shades of one colour — plus one for a toy: a body colour, something
pale for a muzzle or belly, something dark for eyes and details. That one is
chosen by how far each shade stands *off* the body colour rather than by
nearness to an ideal hue, because the nearest pale green to a green body is no
muzzle at all.

Where the honesty shows:

- A neutral base is told that a colour wheel has nothing to say about a
  neutral — every hue sits equally well beside grey.
- A card with nothing in that part of the wheel says so, and names what it
  offered instead, rather than passing off a near-green as a red.
- A shade from the generic palette is refused outright: that palette exists to
  *name* a colour when no card has been captured, not to tell somebody a colour
  they can buy.

Scope decides which cards are in play: this yarn's own card (the default, since
one yarn line behaves consistently in a piece), anything by the same maker, only
yarns in your stash, or every card in the library.

## What to charge

Costs first, then a price, every line shown so it can be argued with. The yarn
comes from what the piece actually takes and the hours from what the work log
recorded, so the price stands on measurements rather than a guess.

Two things people leave out are put back: their own time, and the difference
between a margin and a markup. A 50% markup is a third of the price; a 50%
margin is half of it. Both are worked out and named, because setting one
while meaning the other is the difference between a business and a hobby that
loses money.

Every line is rounded to the penny and the total is the sum of those lines, the
way an invoice works — a column that does not add up when someone checks it
with a calculator is a column they stop trusting.

## The small calculators

- **Spread increases or decreases.** The part people get wrong is that it
  rarely divides: 50 stitches with 6 increases is not six groups of eight, it
  is two of eight and four of seven. The groups are worked out exactly, written
  the way a pattern writes them, and the round can be dropped straight into the
  editor. A decrease that would eat more stitches than the round has is refused
  with the number it could take.
- **Stitches and centimetres**, both directions. Counts are whole stitches, so
  what comes back is what the rounded count really measures, and by how much
  that misses what was asked.
- **Hook and needle sizes**, from the Craft Yarn Council's published table. A
  size that is not on the chart is not quietly rounded into one: the rows
  either side come back instead. The old UK numbers run backwards and predate
  any standard, so they are shown with that warning attached.
- **Units** — metres and yards, grams and ounces — and, for one stated ball,
  length against weight, because a metre of chunky weighs nothing like a metre
  of 4-ply.
- **Saved swatches.** The same yarn on the same hook gives the same gauge next
  time; retyping it is how a piece ends up the wrong size.
- **Granny square blanket.** A blanket is a whole number of squares plus seams
  plus border, so it is never quite the size asked for — the finished
  measurement comes back rather than the wish. At a stated gauge it also works
  out the rounds per square and hands the calculator a real granny-square
  programme, so the whole blanket can be costed in yarn.
